Megatron on a Mesh

A 70B model does not fit on one chip. The way it gets split is older than the hardware and simpler than it looks — it is two matmuls, arranged so that only one of them has to talk.

fractured — a tensor split across chips, each holding a different slice. This is the codebase's word; it avoids "sharded", which TT-NN already uses for splitting across the cores within one chip.

Two things to carry forward. First, the mesh shape is a software view — the same 8 chips can be addressed as (1, 8) or (2, 4), and a 32-chip Galaxy gets reshaped to (4, 8) before it is carved up. Second, the number of rows is what changes the strategy. A mesh with a 1 in its shape is a line, and everything below has one simple form on it. A mesh with both dimensions greater than one is a grid, and the same model has to be split twice.

The idea

There are only two ways to split a matmul

Take one matmul: Y = X @ W, where X is [S, K] — tokens by features — and W is [K, N]. You have two chips and one weight matrix too big for either. You can cut W down its columns, or across its rows. That is the entire design space, and the two choices behave completely differently.

What "partial" actually means

That word does a lot of work later, so it is worth one paragraph. A matmul is a sum: every output element Y[s,n] is a sum over the contracted dimension K. Row-parallel splits K — which means it splits the sum itself. Each chip computes a correct-shaped answer over only its own slice of the terms.

This also explains the naming. all_reduce adds the pieces and leaves the whole result on every chip; reduce_scatter adds them and hands each chip only the slice it will need next. Same addition, different delivery — and since a decoder wants its residual stream fractured anyway, reduce_scatter is usually the right one.

Chaining them cancels the costs

Notice that the two requirements are complements. Column-parallel produces exactly the fractured layout that row-parallel wants. So if you always use them as a pair — column first, row second — the intermediate never needs to be gathered, and you pay a single collective at the very end.

The saving is not subtle. In an MLP the intermediate is the widest tensor in the whole block — for Llama-70B it is 28k wide against a model dim of 8k. Gathering it would move roughly 3.5× more data than gathering the block output. The pairing moves the collective to the narrow end of the graph.

Why the cut in W_qkv is along heads

A column-parallel weight can be cut anywhere along N, but the columns of W_qkv are not interchangeable — they come in groups of head_dim that belong to one head. Cutting on a head boundary means a chip receives the Q, K and V columns of the same heads.

That is what makes the next box free. sdpa only ever mixes a head with itself: scores, softmax and the value-weighted sum all stay inside one head, so if a head lives entirely on one chip the whole attention core runs with no communication. The KV cache is partitioned by head as a side effect, at no extra cost.

# models/tt_transformers/tt/model_config.py:694
self.n_local_heads = self.n_heads // self.cluster_shape[1]

Which is why the config asserts up front that n_heads and n_kv_heads divide evenly by the device count. Split a head across two chips and sdpa would need a collective of its own.

Then W_o maps [n_heads · head_dim] → [H], and its contracted axis is exactly the axis W_qkv just fractured. Cutting it row-wise makes the two weights fit together with nothing in between — the conjugate pair, in a real sublayer.

Sublayer two

MLP, weight by weight

Structurally identical, which is the point — only the dimension being cut changes. Here it is F, the FFN inner dim (args.hidden_dim, 28672 for Llama-70B).

Why mul is free, and what that does not mean

It is tempting to read "no communication" as "the operands must be replicated". The opposite is true. w1 and w3 are cut along F in exactly the same places, so w1_out and w3_out are fractured identically. Element f of one lives on the same chip as element f of the other, and the multiply is therefore chip-local:

# models/tt_transformers/tt/mlp.py:236 — SiLU is fused as an activation on operand A
w2_in = ttnn.mul(w1_out, w3_out, input_tensor_a_activations=[self.activation_type], ...)

The general rule, and it covers the residual add too: an elementwise op costs nothing whenever both operands share a layout. Replicating them would not help and would waste memory. This is also why the residual stream is deliberately left fractured all the way through the layer — it makes both adds free.

And it is why the pairing pays. F is 28672 against a model dim of 8192, so the tensor flowing through mul is the widest thing in the block. Gathering it would move roughly 3.5× what gathering the block output costs. The conjugate pair keeps it fractured and puts the collective at the narrow end.

Both together

The whole layer

Stack the two sublayers and the weights drop away, because by now you know how each one is cut. What is left is the shape of the thing: four collectives, and the state changes only at a collective or at a column-parallel linear.

The residual stream lives fractured

// x is fractured across devices — decoder.py:236
// Norms take fractured inputs and output replicated across devices — :248
// MLP takes replicated inputs and produces fractured outputs — :309

Three comments in the source, and together they are the whole invariant. The norm is the gather — it is the one operation that genuinely needs the full feature vector, because RMSNorm sums over it. The sublayer then fractures the tensor again on its way out.

The gather and the norm can swap places

Both diagrams show all_gather then rms_norm, which is the straightforward reading: you need the whole feature vector before you can normalise over it. A second implementation inverts it — each chip computes partial statistics locally, then one gather combines them (distributed_norm.py:112). Which runs depends on is_distributed_norm(mode): a 2-D mesh always takes it, as does any model with dim > 4096 during prefill.

Either way it is one all-gather per norm moving the same activation, so for the purpose of finding your collectives, treat them as one node.

Those two norm gathers are the largest collectives in the layer. Each moves a full [S, H] activation, while the two reduce-scatters shrink their output as they go. If you are hunting for the expensive collective in a profile, start at the norm, not the matmul.

Two shapes of mesh

Why Galaxy needs six collectives, not four

The diagram above is a line mesh — N300, T3K, a P150x8. For those, four collectives is the whole story and the scheme is Megatron essentially verbatim. A Galaxy inserts two more, at two specific points:

Insert afterExtra collectiveLine mesh
linear W_qkv all_reduce cluster_axis=1 absent
linear w1, w3 reduce_scatter then all_gather, both cluster_axis=1 absent

They really are absent on a line mesh rather than merely cheap — the helper short-circuits before any wire is touched:

# models/tt_transformers/tt/ccl.py:150
# Skip CCL if single device or only 1 device on the target axis
if mesh_shape == [1, 1] or (cluster_axis == 1 and 1 in list(mesh_device.shape)):
    return input_tensor

A T3K opened as (1, 8) has a 1 in its shape, so the post-QKV reduction returns its input untouched. Nothing crosses a wire.

Galaxy has no 1 in (4, 8), and that is deliberate: with 32 chips you want the weights fractured 32 ways, not 8. So each weight is cut on both mesh axes at once — the N dimension on one axis, the K dimension on the other. Splitting K is what creates partials, so whichever axis holds K owes a reduction, and since the four matmuls alternate which axis that is, the collectives alternate with them:

The dims tuples that set this up read as mesh axis → tensor dim, and they flip on Galaxy:

# attention.py:268 · mlp.py:79
dims = (3, 2) if self.TG else (2, 3)      # W_qkv
w1_dims = (-1, -2) if args.is_galaxy else (-2, -1)

So Galaxy trades two extra collectives per layer for a 4× deeper weight split. That is a good trade precisely because those extra reductions run along a short axis — 4 chips, with 4 ethernet links each — while the weight-memory saving applies to the whole 32-chip machine.

The other axis

Data parallelism sits outside all of this

Everything above is tensor parallelism: one model, split across chips, every chip working on the same tokens. Data parallelism is the opposite trade — complete copies of the model, each handling different users.

The two are not interleaved inside a layer. DP carves the physical mesh into disjoint submeshes up front, and each submesh runs the full TP scheme internally:

# models/tt_transformers/tt/generator.py:2954
if num_devices == 32:
    mesh_device.reshape(ttnn.MeshShape(4, 8))
    return mesh_device.create_submeshes(...)

That bottom row is the interesting one. At DP=4 each replica is a (1, 8) line, so the two Galaxy-only collectives disappear and the model runs the simple 1-D scheme — on a Galaxy. Users are routed to a replica by index, model_id = user_id // max_batch_size_per_model (generator.py:707). Tensor parallelism never crosses a submesh boundary.

Prefill is not batched by default

The prefill loop walks users one at a time. What fills the machine during prefill is the sequence itself — thousands of tokens through one replica — not concurrent users. Long prompts are handled by chunking the sequence serially, with a per-model, per-board chunk ceiling (model_config.py:2391): Llama-70B takes 32K chunks on T3K but 128K on Galaxy.

Note this is not Megatron-LM's sequence parallelism, which splits the norm activations along the sequence to save memory. This codebase gathers activations and chunks instead.

Carry these four

What to remember

  1. Column-parallel wants a whole input; row-parallel makes a partial output. Chain them and the costs cancel — one collective per pair, at the narrow end of the graph.
  2. Attention splits by head, which makes SDPA and the KV cache free of communication. MLP splits by hidden dim.
  3. The residual stream lives fractured; the norms are the gathers. If you are hunting for the expensive collective in a profile, look at the norm, not the matmul.
  4. A mesh with a 1 in its shape is a line and gets the simple scheme. A grid gets the weights cut twice and pays two more collectives per layer for a deeper split.
