Tenstorrent Wormhole & Blackhole · kernel performance

Roofline

Two numbers describe what your chip can do; one number describes what your algorithm asks of it. Put them side by side and you know, before writing a line of kernel, which one will stop you.

01 The two ceilings

Every kernel is a race between getting data in and doing arithmetic on it. Each side has a hard limit, and they are measured in different units — which is exactly why they are confusing to compare.

Bandwidth

GB/s

How fast bytes can move from where they live into the core that needs them. Nothing more.

A 195 GB/s DRAM bandwidth means: in one second, 195 billion bytes can cross from DRAM into L1. If your kernel needs 400 GB of traffic, it cannot finish in under two seconds no matter how it is written.

answers: how quickly is the work delivered?

Compute throughput

FLOP/s

How fast arithmetic can be performed once the data has arrived. A FLOP is one floating-point operation; a multiply-accumulate counts as two.

65 TFLOP/s means 65 trillion such operations per second across the whole chip. It says nothing about whether data can arrive fast enough to feed them.

answers: how quickly is the work consumed?

A unit trap worth naming. These get written as GFLOP/s and TFLOP/s almost interchangeably, and a factor of 1000 hides between them. 13,442 GFLOP/s is 13.4 TFLOP/s — the same quantity. When a figure looks absurd, check the prefix before anything else.

Arithmetic intensity is neither of these

The third number is not a hardware property at all. Arithmetic intensity is how much arithmetic your algorithm performs per byte it loads — FLOP/byte. It is a ratio, so it has no “per second” in it and it is not a speed. It describes the shape of a computation: dense algorithms wring many operations out of each byte, sparse ones waste them.

That is what makes it the bridge. Bandwidth is bytes/s, intensity is FLOP/byte, and their product is FLOP/s — the same units as the compute ceiling. Now the two ceilings can be compared.

02 Where the numbers come from

Both ceilings are derivable from the datasheet in a few seconds. Here is where each one actually comes from on our hardware.

Bandwidth — count the DRAM channels

Wormhole N150 Figure Where it comes from
Theoretical 288 GB/s 12 channels of 1 GB GDDR6. FlashAttention.md
Interleaved ~190 GB/s What a matmul actually reads with the default DRAM-interleaved layout — about 66% of theoretical. llms.md
Sharded ~240 GB/s Data split across banks with the nearest core reading each one. llms.md

A measured 195 GB/s on an interleaved kernel sits right where the tech reports say it should. Note the gap between 288 and 190: the theoretical number is almost never the one to plan with. Sharding is a real lever precisely because it recovers part of that gap.

Compute — count cycles per tile, then cores

Each Tensix core has one matrix engine. Per clock cycle it computes an 8×16 × 16×16 matrix product — that is 2 · 8 · 16 · 16 = 4096 multiply-adds every cycle. At 1 GHz that is 4 TFLOP/s per core.

Math fidelity divides it: higher precision costs more passes over the same data. Equivalently, one 32×32×32 tile-matmul (65,536 FLOPs) takes 16 cycles at LoFi and 64 at HiFi4.

peak = cores × 65 536 FLOP/tile ÷ cycles/tile × clock
65.5 TFLOP/s = 64 × 65 536 ÷ 64 × 1 GHz

Wormhole N150 at HiFi4 across the full 8×8 compute grid. Swap the cycles-per-tile figure for another fidelity and you have that ceiling too.

Wormhole vs Blackhole

The matrix engine is identical on both — the tech report states its figures apply to each. What differs is how many of them there are.

Chip Physical grid Compute grid Cores DRAM Peak @ HiFi4
Wormhole N150 8 × 10 8 × 8 64 12 banks · 1 GB 65.5 TFLOP/s
Blackhole 14 × 10 13 × 10 130 8 banks · ~4 GB 133 TFLOP/s

Blackhole has just over twice the compute cores, so roughly twice the arithmetic ceiling at the same clock. Its DRAM bandwidth is not stated in the reports cited here, so the Blackhole ridge is left for you to compute once you have that figure — the recipe is unchanged.

Note the difference between the grids. Wormhole is physically 8×10 but only 8×8 is available for compute; the rest is reserved. Always use the compute grid — compute_with_storage_grid_size() returns it. Using the physical grid inflates your peak by 25% and makes every utilisation figure wrong.

03 Counting intensity

Both ceilings came off the datasheet. The third number comes from your algorithm, and you count it by hand — FLOPs and bytes for one unit of output. Here is the whole napkin, for the naive tile matmul in bfloat16.

The arithmetic

One 32×32 output tile is a sum over Kt tile-matmuls along the inner dimension. Each 32×32×32 tile-matmul is 32³ = 32,768 multiply-accumulates, and a MAC is two FLOPs:

32³ × 2 = 65 536 FLOP per tile-matmul → 65 536 · Kt per output tile

The traffic

To produce that one output tile you read Kt tiles from A and Kt tiles from B. A bfloat16 tile is 32×32×2 bytes:

2 × Kt × 2048 bytes/tile = 4096 · Kt bytes

The ratio

I = 65 536 · Kt / 4096 · Kt = 16 FLOP/byte

Kt appears in both and cancels — doubling the inner dimension doubles the work and the traffic. In general this is 32 / B for B bytes per element: 32 in bfp8, 16 in bfloat16, 8 in float32.

The four assumptions

Every intensity figure is only as good as what it assumed. These are the ones baked into the 16 above, and each is worth checking against your own kernel:

Assumption Why it matters
No reuse Every A and B tile is re-read from DRAM for each output tile. This is the pessimistic case and the one every optimisation attacks — blocking the output raises it several-fold without changing the arithmetic at all.
Output write ignored One tile is written per 2·Kt read, so it is under 2% of traffic at Kt = 32. Include it when Kt is small — for a single-tile reduction it is a third of the traffic.
bfloat16 Intensity scales as 1/B. Halving element width doubles intensity for identical arithmetic, which is why bfp8 is a real lever.
MAC = 2 FLOPs The common convention. Counting MACs instead halves intensity and the ridge point, so the comparison between them is unchanged — but never mix the two.

Compare with element-wise add, same recipe: 1024 additions per output tile against three tiles of traffic (two read, one written) = 1024 / 6144 = 0.17 FLOP/byte. Two orders of magnitude below the matmul, from an algorithm that has no reuse to find at all.

04 Joining them

Bandwidth is bytes/s, intensity is FLOP/byte. Multiply, and the bytes cancel:

195 GB/s × 16 FLOP/byte = 3.1 TFLOP/s
bytes/s × FLOP/byte = FLOP/s

The fastest you could compute if memory were the only limit. Bytes arrive at 195 GB/s and each is worth 16 FLOPs of work.

So the memory ceiling rises with intensity, while the compute ceiling is flat. They cross once. Setting them equal and solving gives the ridge point:

BW × I = peak → I* = peak / BW
I* = 65 536 GFLOP/s / 195 GB/s = 336 FLOP/byte

The machine's own balance, expressed in your algorithm's units so the two are directly comparable. It is a property of the chip, not of anything you write.

That is the whole model: put your algorithm's intensity next to the machine's ridge point. Below it, memory stops you. Above it, arithmetic does.

Try problem size first. Sweep Kt from 1 to 128 and nothing moves. That is not a bug — it is the most useful fact on this page, and section 03 showed the algebra: Kt cancels.

Three things to try

See how far the ridge really is. The default sits at intensity 16 against a ridge of 336 — a factor of 21. Now pick 2-D block and drag to 16: intensity reaches 256, and it is still memory-bound. Reaching the compute roof through DRAM on this chip is genuinely hard.

Watch fidelity do nothing useful. Switch HiFi4 to LoFi. The compute ceiling leaps from 65 to 262 TFLOP/s and the ridge slides right to 1344 — but the amber point does not move at all. Cheaper arithmetic is free when arithmetic was never the constraint, which is exactly why a row-reuse matmul measures the same ~5.2 TFLOP/s at LoFi, HiFi2 and HiFi4.

Change precision instead. Switch bfloat16 to bfp8: intensity doubles, because the arithmetic is unchanged but each tile is half the bytes. Compression is the one lever that moves you right without restructuring the loop.

06 Who owns what

Quantity Owned by What it does
Bandwidth GB/s chip Slope of the memory ceiling. Fixed by the DRAM channels; partly recovered by sharding, multicast, or keeping data in L1.
Chip peak TFLOP/s chip Height of the compute ceiling. Cores × 65,536 ÷ cycles-per-tile × clock.
Ridge point FLOP/byte chip Peak ÷ bandwidth. Where the two ceilings cross, in your algorithm's units.
Intensity FLOP/byte you Your x-coordinate. Set by which algorithm you chose and how much you reuse each loaded byte — not by how well you wrote it.
Problem size neither Scales FLOPs and bytes by the same factor, so the ratio is unchanged. A bigger matmul is not a denser one.
Achieved rate you How close to the ceiling you land. Batching, double buffering and core count live here — they move you up to a roof, never past it.

Size cancels, reuse does not

Section 03 showed Kt cancelling out of the intensity ratio — which is why problem size sets how long an op takes but never which regime it is in. Total FLOPs is 2·M·K·N and grows with every dimension; the ratio does not move.

What does not cancel is reuse. Blocking the output Mb × Nb buys Mb·Nb times the work for (Mb + Nb) times the traffic — a product over a sum. That gap is the entire optimisation.

The row-reuse row is a genuine cross-check: 5.3 TFLOP/s ÷ 193 GB/s = 27.5 FLOPs/byte, recovered from measurement alone. The model and the silicon agree to within a few percent.

Peak is not “best measured”. One widely-quoted benchmark on this board reports 13.4 TFLOP/s — that is an achieved figure on a memory-bound kernel, roughly 20% of the 65.5 TFLOP/s ceiling. Using an achieved number as your ceiling puts the ridge at 69 instead of 336 and makes kernels look far closer to compute-bound than they are. Always derive the ceiling from cores, cycles and clock.

08 Using it

  1. Compute the chip's ridge once for your board and fidelity: cores × 65,536 ÷ cycles-per-tile × clock, divided by bandwidth. Write it on a sticky note. For an N150 at HiFi4 on DRAM it is 336.
  2. Count FLOPs and bytes on paper for one unit of output, before writing the kernel. Divide. That is your x-coordinate.
  3. Compare. Below the ridge means memory-bound; above means compute-bound. You now know your regime before any code exists.
  4. Memory-bound? Optimising arithmetic is wasted effort. Cut bytes: reuse in L1, shard, multicast, drop precision.
  5. Compute-bound? Optimising data movement is wasted effort. Cut work: lower fidelity, better instruction issue.
  6. Measure, then confirm. Bandwidth near its ceiling → memory-bound. FLOP/s near peak → compute-bound. Neither → you are bound by something the roofline has no axis for: latency, dispatch overhead, or load imbalance.

Step 6's third case is the one that catches people. The roofline tells you which ceiling you could hit; hitting neither means the bottleneck is off the chart entirely.
